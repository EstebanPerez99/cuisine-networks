Calling a dish ``authentic'' is one of the strongest compliments a home cook can receive, and one of the most disputed. What makes a recipe authentic is rarely written down. It matters to people all the same: restaurants that reviewers describe as authentic receive higher ratings \cite{kovacs2014authenticity}. Two sources of evidence can still be measured on the same recipes: the ingredients the recipe uses, and the words people use when they review it.

Ahn and colleagues measured the first of these \cite{ahn2011flavor}. For each regional cuisine they asked how much more common an ingredient, or a pair of ingredients, is in that cuisine than in the others, and called this quantity ``authenticity''. In their data, East Asian signatures were dominated by soy sauce, sesame oil, rice and ginger, and North American ones by dairy, eggs and wheat. The same network view has since been applied to other recipe collections \cite{teng2012recipe,jain2015spices}. Ahn's definition is about ingredient lists alone. It does not ask whether the people who review the recipes agree. Here we call the ingredient-based quantity \emph{ingredient distinctiveness} and keep the word ``authentic'' for what reviewers write.

We ask what makes reviewers call a recipe authentic: \textbf{the ingredients it uses, or how it combines them?} The two are easy to confuse. A recipe can use the ingredients that set its cuisine apart, such as soy sauce in a Chinese recipe (\emph{what}). It can also combine its ingredients the way its cuisine usually does, in pairs that appear together more often than the popularity of each ingredient would explain (\emph{how}). Ahn's measure captures the first. If reviewers respond to combinations, a measure of the second should follow their judgments more closely.

We use 26,727 recipes in nine cuisines from Food.com and their 132,744 reviews \cite{majumder2019generating}. We build a recipe--ingredient network, score every recipe for what it uses and for how it combines it, and compare both scores with what reviewers write about the same recipes. Among recipes with screened reviews, higher pairing scores are associated with more frequent authenticity endorsements. The pairing correlation is positive in all nine cuisines, while the ingredient score shows little association.\Endparasplit
