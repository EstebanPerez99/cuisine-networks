Calling a dish ``authentic'' is one of the strongest compliments a home cook can receive, and one of the most disputed. What makes a recipe authentic is rarely written down. It matters to people all the same: restaurants that reviewers describe as authentic receive higher ratings \cite{kovacs2014authenticity}. Two sources of evidence can still be measured on the same recipes: the ingredients the recipe uses, and the words people use when they review it.

Ahn and colleagues measured the first of these \cite{ahn2011flavor}. For each regional cuisine they asked how much more common an ingredient, or a pair of ingredients, is in that cuisine than in the others, and called this quantity ``authenticity''. In their data, East Asian signatures were dominated by soy sauce, sesame oil, rice and ginger, and North American ones by dairy, eggs and wheat. The same network view has since been applied to other recipe collections \cite{teng2012recipe,jain2015spices}. Ahn's definition is about ingredient lists alone. It does not ask whether the people who cook and eat the food agree. Here we call the ingredient-based quantity \emph{ingredient distinctiveness} and keep the word ``authentic'' for what reviewers write.

We ask whether the two agree: \textbf{do the recipes whose ingredients are most distinctive of their cuisine also get called authentic by the people who cook them?} The network view adds a second, sharper question. A recipe can be distinctive because of \emph{what} it contains (ingredients that are common in its cuisine and rare elsewhere) or because of \emph{how} it combines them (pairs that appear together more often than their separate popularity predicts). If reviewers respond to combinations, a pair measure should predict their judgments better than a single-ingredient one.

We use 26,727 recipes in nine cuisines from Food.com and their 132,744 reviews \cite{majumder2019generating}. We build a recipe--ingredient network, give every recipe three ingredient-based scores (what, which pairs, and how unusually it combines them), and compare them with how reviewers talk about the same recipes. Among recipes with screened reviews, stronger pairing scores are associated with more frequent authenticity endorsements. The pairing correlation is positive in all nine cuisines, while single-ingredient scores show little association.\Endparasplit
